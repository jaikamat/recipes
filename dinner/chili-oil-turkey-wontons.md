### Chili Oil Turkey Wontons

**Servings:** 4
**Per serving:** 505 cal | 28g protein | 24g fat | 44g carbs

Silky turkey wontons in a glossy Szechuan-style chili oil that's spicy, fruity, sweet, and tongue-buzzing. Makes about 35 wontons (8 per serving, with a few spare); macros assume 8 wontons and ~20g of the concentrated chili oil per serving.

**Ingredients:**

_Wontons:_

- 35 square wonton wrappers (about 280g)
- 454g (1 lb) ground turkey (93/7)
- 1 egg (50g)
- 50g Kirkland chicken bone broth, cold
- 10g (2 tsp) garlic paste
- 10g (1 TBSP) ginger paste
- 25g (about 3 TBSP) scallions, sliced
- 10g soy sauce
- 10g sesame oil
- 10g (2 tsp) sea salt
- 5g granulated sugar
- 4g white pepper, ground

_Chili Oil:_

- 200g (about 7/8 cup) peanut oil
- 20g gochugaru chile powder, divided into two 10g portions
- 10g chili flakes
- 15g sesame seeds
- 2g Szechuan peppercorns, ground
- 20g (4 tsp) garlic paste
- 50g honey
- 30g soy sauce
- 15g Chinese black vinegar
- 2g MSG

_For serving:_

- scallions, sliced (optional)

**Instructions:**

1. In a heat-resistant bowl set next to the stove, combine 10g of the gochugaru with the chili flakes, sesame seeds, Szechuan peppercorns, and garlic paste
2. In a small saucepan, combine the peanut oil and remaining 10g gochugaru. Heat over medium to 350°F, stirring occasionally, until the gochugaru sizzles steadily and the oil turns deep orange-red, 4–5 minutes
3. Carefully strain the hot oil into the bowl of dry ingredients — it will sizzle aggressively — and stir gently so everything blooms evenly
4. Let the oil cool until the side of the bowl is no hotter than a hot coffee cup (adding the liquids to ripping-hot oil makes it spit), then stir in the honey, black vinegar, soy sauce, and MSG. Set aside
5. In a large bowl, combine the turkey, egg, garlic paste, ginger paste, scallions, soy sauce, sesame oil, salt, sugar, and white pepper. Whip aggressively by hand for 4–5 minutes (or stand mixer with paddle, medium speed, 2–3 minutes) until the meat turns from loose ground turkey into a sticky, paste-like mass — this myosin development keeps the filling springy and bonded to the wrapper. Then whip in the cold bone broth a splash at a time until fully absorbed — the sticky meat traps the liquid, and it's what keeps the lean filling juicy
6. Keep the wrapper stack under a damp paper towel while you work. Place 15g (1 scant TBSP) of filling in the center of a wrapper, wet the edges, and fold corner to corner into a triangle, pressing out all air as you seal. Crimp the edges 5–6 times, gathering the folds up like a beggar's purse, and squeeze to seal. Place finished wontons on a parchment-lined sheet tray
7. Cover the tray and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes — the wrapper pulls moisture from the filling and bonds to it, so rested wontons hold together in the water instead of splitting
8. Bring a medium pot of well-salted water to a high simmer, 190–200°F — steady bubbles, not a rolling boil, which tears wrappers and squeezes the juice out of the filling. Add wontons in batches of 12–15, swirling each batch in the water for about 10 seconds before releasing to prevent sticking. Cook 4 minutes, then lift out with a spider strainer, dab off excess water, and divide into bowls
9. Whisk 30g of the starchy poaching water into the ~80g of chili oil you're using for this batch — the starch emulsifies it into a glossy glaze that clings to the wontons. Only add water to the portion of oil you're using now; it keeps better without it
10. Spoon the chili oil generously over the wontons, making sure to scoop solids from the bottom of the bowl where most of the flavor lives. Garnish with scallions

**Freeze:** Freeze raw wrapped wontons flat on a cornstarch-dusted or parchment-lined tray until solid, about 2 hours, then transfer to a zip-top bag. Keeps 2–3 months. Cook straight from frozen at the same gentle simmer, adding 1–2 minutes — don't thaw first or they turn gummy and stick together.

**Serve:** With steamed rice to stretch the portion and soak up extra oil. Steamed bok choy or gai lan cuts the richness.

**Storage:** The chili oil (without poaching water added) keeps in a sealed jar in the fridge for several months — spoon it over rice bowls, fried eggs, ramen, or dumplings. Cooked wontons don't reheat well (gummy wrapper, overcooked filling), so boil only what you'll eat and keep the rest frozen raw.

**Notes:**

- 80/20 ground pork (the original) adds ~75 cal and ~9g fat per serving; ground chicken thigh sits in between (~+35 cal, +4g fat). With either, skip the bone broth whip-in — the fat does that job
- The whipped-in broth is non-negotiable with 93/7 turkey; without it the lean filling cooks up dry and crumbly
- The chili oil uses half the original oil with the same solids, honey, and seasonings, so it's twice as concentrated — a little goes further. Dropping the dose to 15g per serving shaves ~30 cal and ~3g fat if you're cutting hard
- The full chili oil batch makes ~350g, more than one round of wontons needs — that's on purpose
- Splitting the gochugaru keeps the oil saucy instead of sludgy: half infuses the oil and gets strained out, half stays in the finished oil
- Skipping the MSG? Add 3g sea salt to keep the balance
- Balsamic is the closest sub for Chinese black vinegar; rice vinegar works in a pinch but loses depth
- No substitute for Szechuan peppercorns — black pepper is tasty but won't give the numbing má tingle
