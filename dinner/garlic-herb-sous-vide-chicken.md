### Garlic Herb Sous Vide Chicken Breast

**Servings:** 2 (about 250g each)
**Per serving:** 396 cal | 57g protein | 17g fat | 1g carbs

Sous vide at 146.5°F, seasoned entirely with dried spices — no sprigs to fish out
of the bag, and garlic powder instead of fresh cloves, which turn harsh over a
long bath. Finished with a hard butter sear.

**Ingredients:**

_Seasoning:_

- 2g sea salt
- 1.5g garlic powder
- 1.2g celery salt
- 1g paprika
- 0.5g dried thyme
- 0.5g dried rosemary
- black pepper, to taste

_Chicken:_

- 500g chicken breast (2 thick breasts)
- 9g olive oil
- 14g butter

**Instructions:**

1. Set the sous vide circulator to 146.5°F and let it come up to temperature
2. Mix all the seasoning ingredients in a small bowl and rub over both breasts, coating every surface
3. Pour the olive oil into the sous vide bag, folding the rim of the bag over before you load the chicken so nothing fouls the seal
4. Lay the breasts flat in a single layer and seal moderately snug — not crushed, since compressed meat cooks unevenly
5. Cook 2 hours for thick breasts (1.5 inches or more at the fat end), or 1 hour for standard breasts around 1 inch
6. Pull the bag and get the chicken out immediately — don't let it sit in the bag, which traps steam against the surface and works against the sear
7. Pat both sides completely dry, right down to a matte surface. Moisture is the only thing standing between you and a crust
8. Heat butter in a skillet over medium-high until it stops foaming and smells nutty, then sear 30-60 seconds per side, pressing down lightly for full contact
9. Rest 1-2 minutes off the heat, then slice and serve

**Freeze:** Best raw — season and bag through step 4, then freeze the sealed bag flat on a sheet pan so it sets as an even slab. Once solid, stack upright in the freezer for up to 3 months. Cooked breasts also freeze: ice-bath the sealed bag for 20 minutes straight out of the water bath, then freeze unseared.

**Thaw:** Don't. Raw bags go into the bath straight from the freezer (see below). Cooked bags thaw overnight in the fridge, still sealed.

**Reheat:** Cooked-and-frozen bags go into a 140°F bath for 45 minutes from frozen, or 30 minutes from thawed, then dry and sear per steps 7-8. Microwaving cooked sous vide chicken overshoots and dries it out.

**Serve:** Sliced over Greek lemon rice, or chilled and sliced into salads.

**Storage:** In the sealed bag in the fridge up to 4 days if ice-bath chilled first, 2 days if not.

**Notes:** 146.5°F gives a juicy, faintly pink-at-the-center breast that is fully
pasteurized — sous vide holds the meat at temperature long enough that safety
comes from time at 146.5°F, not from hitting 165°F. If you want a firmer,
more conventional texture, go 150-155°F instead.

Two hours is well within the safe and pleasant window. Time in a sous vide bath
is driven by thickness, not weight: a 1-inch breast needs about an hour to reach
core temperature, a 1.5-inch breast closer to two. Everything past that point is
holding, not cooking, and pasteurization at this temperature is finished within
minutes of the core arriving. The real limit is texture, not safety — chicken
breast starts going mealy and shreddy somewhere past 4 hours, so 2 hours has
plenty of headroom on both sides.

From frozen, for weeknights: drop the frozen raw bag straight into the preheated
146.5°F bath and add 50% to the time — 3 hours for thick breasts, 1.5 hours for
standard 1-inch ones. Everything from step 6 onward is unchanged. No thawing, and
no safety penalty: the bath drives the meat through the 40-140°F danger zone
faster than a countertop thaw would, and the bag is already at final temperature
the whole time.

Three things make or break this. **Freeze the bag flat** on a sheet pan before
stacking it — a curled or lumpy bag cooks unevenly and blows up the timing, since
thickness is what the clock is really measuring. **One breast per bag, not
touching**, or two overlapping breasts freeze into one thick mass that needs far
longer than either would alone. And **weigh the bag down**, since frozen bags
float and any part above the waterline isn't cooking.

Worth pounding very thick breasts to an even thickness before freezing. Three
hours is fine, but it's the upper end of this window — past about 4 hours the
texture starts turning mealy, and evening out the thickness buys back an hour
while also fixing the tapered-end problem where the thin end overcooks.

Label the bag with the date and the cook time it needs, not just the date. The
whole point is not having to re-measure a frozen breast on a weeknight.

Dried thyme and rosemary are used at roughly a third the volume of the fresh
sprigs, since they infuse more aggressively in a sealed bag than loose sprigs do.
Skip the celery salt if you don't stock it and bump the sea salt to 2.5g.

Fresh herb version, as the original recipe runs it: leave the dried thyme and
rosemary out of the rub and instead lay 1 sprig fresh rosemary and 1-2 sprigs
fresh thyme in the bag alongside the chicken, then discard them before searing.
The sprigs go in whole and loose rather than rubbed on — intact leaves release
their oils slowly into the warm fat, which gives a rounder, less resinous flavor
than dried rosemary does. It's the one place fresh genuinely beats dried here,
since a sealed bag at 146.5°F is close to a gentle herb infusion. Macros are
unchanged either way.

On resting: rest after the sear, not before, and never in the bag. Conventional
resting exists to even out a steep temperature gradient — a roasted breast is
200°F outside and 140°F in the middle. Sous vide chicken comes out uniform at
146.5°F, so there's no gradient to settle and no carryover to wait out. The only
genuinely hot part is the few millimeters under the crust after searing, which is
what the 1-2 minutes in step 9 is for. Resting won't reclaim juices either; the
purge already happened in the bag during the cook.
